Cooking Notes: Heat, Salt, Texture, and Timing
Cooking is applied observation. A recipe gives a useful path, but ingredients vary in size, moisture, temperature, and freshness. Learning to notice smell, sound, color, and texture makes recipes easier to adapt.

A pan of onions does not become browned because a timer reaches a certain number. It browns when enough water has cooked away and the surface temperature allows browning reactions to occur. A pot of pasta is not done because the box says ten minutes; it is done when the texture is right for the dish.

Build flavor in layers
Many savory dishes begin with a sequence:

Heat fat in a pan.

Add aromatic ingredients such as onion, garlic, celery, or ginger.

Season lightly early, then adjust later.

Add a main ingredient and let it develop color.

Deglaze with water, stock, wine, or another liquid.

Simmer until the flavors combine.

Finish with acid, herbs, fat, or fresh seasoning.

Each stage changes the flavor. Browning adds roasted notes. Simmering distributes flavor through a sauce. Acid can make a finished dish taste brighter. Fresh herbs added near the end preserve a different character than herbs cooked from the beginning.

Temperature and texture
A thermometer is useful because it replaces guesswork with a measurement. It is particularly valuable for roasting meat, baking bread, and heating sugar syrups. But a thermometer does not replace judgment. A chicken thigh can be safe at one temperature and still benefit from additional cooking to become tender. Bread may reach its target internal temperature but need more crust color.

Texture comes from structure. Starch thickens sauces. Protein firms as it cooks. Fat carries flavor and changes mouthfeel. Water can steam, boil, evaporate, or remain trapped in food. When a dish turns out poorly, identify the structure that changed rather than only describing it as "bad."

Taste deliberately
Taste food during cooking when it is safe to do so. Ask focused questions:

Is it salty enough?

Does it need acidity?

Is the texture too thick or too thin?

Are the flavors balanced?

Would fresh contrast help: lemon, herbs, yogurt, or a crisp vegetable?

Cooking becomes more reliable when you make one adjustment at a time. A small pinch of salt can be evaluated. Adding salt, lemon, chili, and sugar all at once makes it difficult to know which change helped.
